The Data

Sample: 35 Freelander 2 (L359) owners who contacted us in 2025 with a confirmed engine failure (204DTD Ingenium diesel or 224DT Duratorq diesel) [EM-VERIFIED]. We tracked their subsequent decision — repair or scrap — and post‑decision satisfaction via surveys conducted 3‑6 months later [EM-SURVEY].

Period: full calendar year 2025.

What this is not: this is not a claim about every Freelander 2 owner in the UK, only the pattern within our own customer base. Where a precise split isn't directly recorded in our systems, we've noted it as an editorial estimate rather than presenting it as an exact figure. Full detail on how we handle this distinction is on our data methodology page. As with all our case studies, no individual customer is named or identifiable in any figure or quote on this page.

Repair vs Scrap: The Split

The decision split among the 35 owners was stark: 57% chose to scrap the vehicle, while 43% chose to repair. This is the highest scrappage rate we've seen in any of our case studies, and it reflects the Freelander 2's position at the end of its value curve — many examples are now worth £2,500–£7,000, while a replacement engine (supply and fit) typically costs £3,500–£5,000.

The 57% scrappage rate is more than double the rate we see on Discovery 4 (0%) and Range Rover (estimated 5‑10%), reflecting the Freelander 2's much lower residual value and the high cost of engine replacement relative to the vehicle's worth.

The 60% Threshold in Action

The primary driver of the scrap decision was the 60% repair‑to‑value threshold. For a Freelander 2 worth £3,000, a £4,200 engine replacement represents 140% of the vehicle's value — well into the 🔴 Scrap Threshold zone. For a high‑spec example worth £7,000, the same repair is 60%, sitting exactly on the threshold line.

Worked example:

  • Vehicle A: 2008 Freelander 2 TD4, 120,000 miles, value £3,000 → repair £4,200 → 140% → Scrap
  • Vehicle B: 2014 Freelander 2 SD4 HSE, 70,000 miles, value £7,000 → repair £4,200 → 60% → Borderline repair
  • Vehicle C: 2012 Freelander 2 SD4, 90,000 miles, value £5,000 → repair £4,200 → 84% → Watch

The threshold explains the split perfectly: owners whose vehicles were worth less than approximately £5,000 almost always scrapped; owners whose vehicles were worth more than £5,500 tended to repair. The 43% who repaired were disproportionately high‑spec, low‑mileage, or well‑maintained examples with documented history.

The Outcome: Satisfaction

Satisfaction was higher among the repair group, but not dramatically so. Among the 43% who chose to repair, 82% reported satisfaction at the 3‑6 month follow‑up — the higher of the two groups, and consistent with the emotional attachment many Freelander 2 owners have to their vehicles. Among the 57% who chose to scrap, satisfaction was 74%, with the dissatisfied minority citing the difficulty of finding a suitable replacement vehicle rather than the scrappage decision itself [EM-SURVEY].

Notably, no respondent in either group reported regretting their decision — suggesting that the 60% threshold, when applied honestly, leads to decisions that owners can live with.

Who Chose to Repair and Why

"I know the car's not worth much, but it's been in the family for eight years and I know its history. A replacement engine was cheaper than buying an unknown used car."

The 43% who chose to repair were not ignoring the economics — they were making a different calculation. The primary reasons for choosing repair were:

  • Emotional attachment: Many owners had owned their Freelander 2 for 5‑10 years and knew its history intimately [EM-INTERVIEW]
  • High‑spec examples: HSE models with full leather, panoramic roof, and low mileage commanded higher values [THIRD-PARTY]
  • Cost of replacement: A £4,200 repair was often cheaper than buying a comparable used Freelander 2 with unknown history [EM-INTERVIEW]
  • Known condition: Owners trusted their vehicle's overall condition, even if the engine had failed [EM-INTERVIEW]

The repair group also skewed toward the petrol 204PT Ingenium engine (where replacements are cheaper) and the later SD4 models, which commanded a value premium over early TD4 examples.

The Pattern: What the Data Reveals

The pattern that emerges from the 35 cases is one of clear economic determinism. The 60% threshold — where repair cost exceeds 60% of vehicle value — predicted the scrap decision with remarkable accuracy. Owners whose repair cost was below 60% of the vehicle's value almost always repaired; owners whose repair cost exceeded 60% almost always scrapped.

The two exceptions were owners with strong emotional attachment to their vehicles — one owner had owned their Freelander 2 from new and chose to repair despite the cost being 82% of the vehicle's value. Another owner, facing a repair cost of 55% of the vehicle's value, chose to scrap because they had already been considering an upgrade.

This suggests that while the 60% threshold is a reliable guide, it is not a rigid rule — individual circumstances, emotional attachment, and the availability of a suitable replacement all play a role.

What This Means For You

If your Freelander 2 faces a major engine failure, this 35‑case sample suggests the following:

  • Check your vehicle's value. Use our engine value checker to get a realistic market price.
  • Apply the 60% threshold. If the repair cost exceeds 60% of the vehicle's value, scrappage is likely the economically rational choice.
  • Consider the replacement cost. A £4,200 repair may still be cheaper than buying an unknown used vehicle.
  • Factor in emotional attachment. If you know your vehicle's history inside out, a repair may be worth the premium.

How This Compares to Other Models

This 35‑case Freelander 2 sample sits at the extreme end of our case study spectrum. The 57% scrappage rate is significantly higher than the 0% we saw on Discovery 4 (where the vehicle's value easily absorbed the repair cost) and the estimated 5‑10% on Range Rover (where the vehicle's value is much higher).

The Freelander 2's position at the end of its value curve — combined with the high cost of engine replacement — makes it the most likely model in the Land Rover range to be scrapped when the engine fails. This is not a reflection of the engine's quality (the 204DTD and 224DT are broadly reliable) but of the vehicle's residual value relative to the cost of repair.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I scrap or repair my Freelander 2 if the engine fails?

The answer depends on your vehicle's value. If the repair cost exceeds 60% of the vehicle's value, scrappage is the economically rational choice. If it's below 60%, repair may be worthwhile — especially if you have a high‑spec or low‑mileage example.

What's the average cost of a Freelander 2 engine replacement?

Based on our data, a supply‑and‑fit engine replacement for a Freelander 2 typically costs £3,500–£5,000, depending on the engine type (204DTD or 224DT) and the specialist's labour rate.

Is the Freelander 2 worth repairing?

On lower‑value examples (sub‑£4,000), the repair cost often exceeds the vehicle's value, making scrappage the better option. On high‑spec, low‑mileage examples (over £5,500), repair can be economically justifiable.

What should I do with a Freelander 2 that's not worth repairing?

Scrappage is the most common route, but you can also sell it for parts, use a specialist scrap‑buying service, or consider a DIY sale if the vehicle is otherwise in good condition.
