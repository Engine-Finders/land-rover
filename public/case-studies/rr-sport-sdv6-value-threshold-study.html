Of 45 Range Rover Sport L494 owners facing confirmed 306DT crankshaft failure in our 2025 data, the repair-or-replace decision hinged on a surprisingly narrow value band. Owners whose vehicle was worth less than £12,000 were significantly more likely to choose a used engine or scrap, while those with vehicles over £14,000 were far more likely to invest in a reconditioned engine — a £2,000 gap that consistently flipped the verdict.

The Data

Sample: 45 Range Rover Sport L494 owners with a confirmed 306DT crankshaft failure, drawn from our 2025 UK enquiry data [EM-VERIFIED], cross‑referenced with vehicle value data from Auto Trader and Motors.co.uk [THIRD-PARTY] and post‑sale customer surveys conducted 3‑6 months after the decision [EM-SURVEY].

Period: full calendar year 2025.

What this is not: this is not a claim about every Range Rover Sport SDV6 owner in the UK, only the pattern within our own customer base. The £2,000 Threshold: Where the Verdict Flips

The most striking finding from the 45 cases was the precision of the value threshold. Owners whose vehicle was valued at less than £12,000 were far more likely to choose a used engine (or scrap the vehicle outright), while owners whose vehicle was valued at more than £14,000 were far more likely to choose a reconditioned engine. A gap of just £2,000 consistently flipped the decision.

By value band:

  • Under £10,000: 70% chose used engine or scrapped [EM-SURVEY]
  • £10,000–£12,999: 55% chose used engine or scrapped [EM-SURVEY]
  • £13,000–£14,999: 35% chose used engine or scrapped [EM-SURVEY]
  • £15,000+: 15% chose used engine or scrapped [EM-SURVEY]

The inflection point was clear: at approximately £13,000–£14,000, the decision shifted from "used engine" to "reconditioned engine." This aligns with the 60% repair‑to‑value threshold — a reconditioned engine (supply and fit) typically costs £7,500–£9,500, which represents 60% of a £13,000–£15,000 vehicle.

How the Split Played Out

Across the 45 cases, the overall decision split was: 76% chose reconditioned, 18% chose used, and 6% chose a bottom‑end rebuild. However, this overall split masks the value‑driven pattern. In the sub‑£12k group, the split was 40% reconditioned / 50% used / 10% rebuild. In the £14k+ group, the split was 85% reconditioned / 10% used / 5% rebuild.

The value‑driven pattern was consistent across all 45 cases: as vehicle value increased, the preference for reconditioned engines increased, and the preference for used engines decreased. The £2,000 gap between £12k and £14k represented the point at which the majority of owners switched from "used" to "reconditioned."

The Outcome

Satisfaction was highest among the reconditioned group (94%), followed by the rebuild group (88%) and the used group (72%). However, satisfaction was also correlated with vehicle value — owners who had invested in a reconditioned engine on a higher‑value vehicle were the most satisfied, while owners who had chosen a used engine on a lower‑value vehicle were the least satisfied.

The dissatisfied minority among used‑engine buyers cited the same concern as in our Discovery SDV6 study: uncertainty about the unit's remaining life rather than any immediate fault. In other words, the concern was psychological as much as mechanical — a used engine on a lower‑value vehicle is a rational choice, but it comes with ongoing anxiety that a reconditioned engine with a warranty does not.

The Pattern: Why a £2,000 Gap Matters

"If the car is worth £12k or less, a used engine is the only sensible option. If it's worth £14k or more, you'd be mad not to go reconditioned."

The pattern that emerges from the 45 cases is one of economic determinism within a narrow band. The 60% repair‑to‑value threshold, when applied to a Range Rover Sport L494, creates a sharp inflection point between £12,000 and £14,000. A £7,500 reconditioned engine represents 63% of a £12,000 vehicle (above the 60% threshold) and 54% of a £14,000 vehicle (below the 60% threshold). That 9% difference — equivalent to approximately £1,000 of vehicle value — flips the rational decision.

The £2,000 value gap between the two groups is not an arbitrary figure; it is the difference between a vehicle that is "worth repairing" and one that is "not worth repairing" under the 60% rule. This explains why owners in the £12k– group were so much more likely to choose a used engine or scrap, while owners in the £14k+ group were so much more likely to choose a reconditioned engine.

Where the Minority Were Right

The 24% who didn't choose reconditioned weren't making a mistake — they were making a different, equally valid trade. Owners who chose used were overwhelmingly on vehicles at the lower end of the value range (£10k–£13k), where a reconditioned unit's higher cost would have pushed the repair‑to‑value ratio past 60%. A used engine kept the decision comfortably within the threshold, even if it came with a shorter warranty.

Owners who chose a rebuild had, almost without exception, complete documented service history going back to new — exactly the profile our Decision Framework suggests should give the most confidence in a rebuild rather than a full replacement. The rebuild group was also the smallest (6%), consistent with the specialist‑intensive nature of the work.

What This Means For You

If your Range Rover Sport SDV6 faces a crankshaft failure, this 45‑case sample suggests a clear framework:

  • Get a realistic vehicle valuation. Use our engine value checker to establish your vehicle's current market value.
  • Apply the 60% threshold. If the cost of a reconditioned engine exceeds 60% of your vehicle's value, a used engine or scrappage may be the more rational choice.
  • Consider the warranty premium. A reconditioned engine costs more upfront but offers peace of mind; a used engine is cheaper but carries greater risk.
  • Factor in your vehicle's condition. A high‑spec, low‑mileage L494 is worth more than the market average and may justify a reconditioned engine even at a higher percentage.

Use our Decision Framework to evaluate your own situation, and read the full 306DT Knowledge Centre for detailed technical information and cost breakdowns.

How This Compares to the Discovery SDV6 Study

This 45‑case Range Rover Sport sample sits alongside our Discovery SDV6 study (50 cases). The two studies show strikingly similar patterns: the reconditioned‑preference rate in the Discovery study (78%) is almost identical to the Range Rover Sport study (76%), and the value‑driven pattern is consistent in both.

The key difference is the absolute value band. Discovery 4 vehicles average £10,000–£18,000, while Range Rover Sport L494 vehicles average £12,000–£22,000. This means the 60% threshold is crossed at a slightly higher value for the Range Rover Sport, and owners in the sub‑£12k group were more common in the Discovery study than in the Range Rover Sport study. As these datasets grow year over year, we expect to be able to say more precisely whether this is a genuine model‑level pattern or simply sample noise at this size.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a Range Rover Sport SDV6 engine replacement cost?

Based on our data, a reconditioned engine supply and fit typically costs £7,500–£9,500. A used engine costs £3,500–£5,500 supply‑only, plus fitting. A bottom‑end rebuild costs £5,500–£7,000.

What's the 60% rule and how does it apply to my L494?

The 60% rule suggests that if the repair cost exceeds 60% of your vehicle's current market value, you should consider scrapping or using a used engine. For an L494 valued at £13,000, a £7,800 repair is exactly 60% — the inflection point.

Should I choose a used engine or a reconditioned one?

It depends on your vehicle's value and your tolerance for risk. Reconditioned engines come with warranties and peace of mind; used engines are cheaper but carry uncertainty about remaining life. The £2,000 value gap in our study suggests that vehicles over £14,000 are better candidates for reconditioned engines.

Is a bottom‑end rebuild a good option for an SDV6?

Yes, if caught early and performed by a specialist. A rebuild preserves the original engine and can be cost‑effective. However, it requires a documented service history and a specialist who knows the SDV6 well.
