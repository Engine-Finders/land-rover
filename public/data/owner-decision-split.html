Methodology

How we built this decision‑split report from real UK enquiry data.

Source: Internal CRM quote‑request data from EnginesMarket.co.uk and EngineFinders.co.uk, combined. Full calendar year 2025. 24,650 unique quote requests across all brands; 4,935 of these relate specifically to Land Rover and Range Rover engines.

What counts as a “decision”: every enquiry represents an owner actively seeking a replacement engine. The condition they request (reconditioned, used, or rebuilt) and the repair‑to‑value flag derived from our data indicate whether they ultimately repaired, replaced, or scrapped the vehicle.

Repair‑to‑Value threshold: we apply a 60% threshold for Land Rover and Jaguar, meaning if the lowest viable replacement cost exceeds 60% of the vehicle’s current market value, scrapping is often the financially sensible choice. This is based on industry consensus and our own data [THIRD‑PARTY].

Flag definitions: ✅ Safe = replacement cost well within vehicle value; ⚠️ Watch = cost may be 40–70% of value; 🔴 Scrap Threshold = cost risks exceeding value – evaluate scrap first.

Refresh cycle: annually, each February, covering the prior full calendar year. Figures are locked at publication and not silently revised.

What Owners Actually Chose

Aggregating the model‑level data reveals the real‑world decision split.

Of the Land Rover models with complete enquiry data and decision flags, the distribution of owner decisions by financial viability is:

✅ Safe
~60%
⚠️ Watch
~32%
🔴 Scrap Threshold
~8%

These percentages are based on the subset of models with both enquiry counts and decision flags [ESTIMATED]. The majority of owners – over 60% – are in the Safe category, meaning replacement cost is comfortably within the vehicle’s value. Around 32% fall into the Watch zone, where careful evaluation is needed. The Scrap category is dominated by the Freelander 2, where the cost of a replacement engine often exceeds the car’s market value.

Notably, reconditioned engines were the preferred choice across all brands (70.45% of enquiries [EM‑VERIFIED]), and for Land Rover engines, the typical condition was almost always reconditioned – only the 224DT (used in Freelander and early Evoque) was most commonly requested as used. This suggests that owners and specialists alike favour the reliability and warranty of a professionally rebuilt unit over a used engine of unknown history.

What This Means for You

If your Land Rover has suffered an engine failure, the decision to repair, replace, or scrap hinges on the 60% repair‑to‑value threshold. Our data shows that for most modern Land Rover models (L405, L494, L663, L551, etc.), replacement is financially sound – the cost of a reconditioned engine is well within the vehicle’s value. For older vehicles like the Freelander 2, the calculation often tips towards scrapping.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 60% repair‑to‑value threshold?

We recommend that if the lowest viable replacement cost exceeds approximately 60% of your vehicle’s current market value, you should evaluate scrapping or selling as a non‑runner. This threshold is based on industry consensus and our own data analysis.

Which Land Rover models are safest to repair?

Models like the Range Rover L405, Range Rover Sport L494, Defender L663, and Evoque L551 all fall into the ✅ Safe category – replacement cost is well within vehicle value.

Why is the Freelander 2 flagged as Scrap Threshold?

The Freelander 2’s market value is often £2,000–£5,000, while a reconditioned 204DTD engine costs £3,500–£5,800, frequently exceeding the vehicle’s worth. Many owners choose to scrap rather than replace.
