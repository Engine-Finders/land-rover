The short answer: yes, if the fitted replacement cost is less than 60% of your Defender's current market value. For an L663 Defender, that's almost always yes — values are high enough to absorb even a £7,000–£9,500 engine replacement. For a Classic Defender, the answer is often yes too, because values have risen sharply. The most marginal case is the L316 (pre-2016) Defender, where values vary from £8,000–£25,000 and the answer depends on the exact example.

How the 60% Rule Decides Whether to Replace a Defender Engine

This is the single most important number in any Defender ownership decision. Here is how it works, why it exists, and how to apply it to your own vehicle.

The calculation is simple: divide the full supply-and-fit cost of the replacement engine by your Defender's current market value. If the result is below 60%, the repair is financially sensible. Above 60%, the honest verdict is usually to scrap the vehicle or sell it for parts.

Why 60% for Land Rover and Defender? Our enquiry data [EM-VERIFIED] shows that owners who spend less than 60% of their vehicle's value on an engine replacement are substantially more likely to report the decision as worthwhile a year later. Above that line, the split shifts sharply toward regret or early re-sale. The threshold is set deliberately conservative — slightly below the point our data suggests most owners actually break even — to account for the uncertainty in any single vehicle valuation.

What about the 70% threshold used by BMW and Mercedes? BMW and Mercedes-Benz sit at 70% in our data, reflecting a higher typical parts and labour baseline for those brands. The Defender threshold is 60% because the parts and labour baseline in our enquiry data is genuinely different — not because one brand is "better" than another. Full detail on this is on our Repair, Replace or Scrap Framework page.

The most common mistake: using the original purchase price or the car's value five years ago. The calculation only works against today's trade or private sale value. A Classic Defender bought for £5,000 ten years ago may now be worth £15,000 — the ratio changes dramatically.

Key rule for Defender owners:

The 60% threshold is a starting guide, not an absolute cut-off. A rot-free Classic Defender with original panels may justify a repair above the threshold. A tired L316 with significant corrosion probably won't clear it even below. Always assess the whole vehicle.

Defender Engine Replacement — By Generation

Each Defender generation has a different value profile and different engine risks. This is how the 60% threshold applies across the range.

✅

Defender Classic (1983–2006) — 300Tdi, Td5, V8

Typical value: £8,000–£25,000 [ESTIMATED]

Primary engines: 300Tdi · Td5 · 3.5/3.9/4.0 V8

Recon supply costs: £2,500–£5,500 [EM-QUOTE]

R-to-V verdict: ✅ Safe on most examples

The Classic Defender is a special case. Strong residual values mean even a £5,000 fitted repair on a £10,000 vehicle is 50% — safely below the threshold. The 300Tdi is robust and rebuildable, the Td5 is well-understood, and the V8s have simple parts availability. The key risk is not the engine cost but the vehicle's overall condition — a rot-free, original-panel Classic Defender is a much better investment candidate than a heavily corroded example.

Defender L316 (2006–2016) — 2.4/2.2 TDCi

Typical value: £8,000–£18,000 [ESTIMATED]

Primary engines: 2.4 TDCi · 2.2 TDCi (Duratorq)

Recon supply costs: £2,000–£3,500 [EM-QUOTE]

R-to-V verdict: ⚠️ Watch — depends on value

The L316 is the most marginal generation. Values range from £8,000 for a high-mileage 2.2 TDCi to £18,000 for a low-mileage, well-specced example. A £3,000–£4,500 fitted repair on a £9,000 vehicle is 33–50% — safe. The same repair on an £8,000 vehicle is 38–56% — still safe, but closer to the threshold. The key is the vehicle's condition — rot is the real killer of L316 Defenders, not engine failure. Our Defender D200 chain case study explores this in detail.

✅

Defender L663 (2020–present) — D200/D240/D300/P400

Typical value: £35,000–£90,000 [ESTIMATED]

Primary engines: 204DTD (D200/D240) · 306DT (D300/D350) · P400 Ingenium Petrol

Recon supply costs: £3,500–£9,500 [EM-QUOTE]

R-to-V verdict: ✅ Very safe

The newest generation is the safest of all. L663 values are so high that even the most expensive engine replacement (£9,500 supply, £11,500 fitted) is only 13–33% of the vehicle's value. The 204DTD Ingenium diesel is the same engine found in the Discovery Sport and Evoque, with the same timing chain caveats — but the financial buffer is so large that the economics page is almost irrelevant. The decision is about reliability and availability, not cost.

When Replacing a Defender Engine Is Not Worth It

A site that always recommends replacement is a sales funnel wearing an editorial costume. Sometimes the right answer is to walk away, and we say so plainly.

🚫

High-Mileage L316 with Significant Corrosion

Late in its value curve, a 2.2 TDCi replacement may be 50-60% of the vehicle's value. Combined with chassis rot, bulkhead corrosion and tired suspension, the honest advice is usually to walk away. Our When Should You Scrap a Land Rover? page covers this in detail.

Typical fitted cost: £2,800–£4,500 [EM-QUOTE]

Typical L316 value: £8,000–£12,000 [ESTIMATED]

R-to-V: 23–56% — safe on good examples, but 🔴 Scrap if corrosion is severe

🚫

Classic Defender with Extensive Rot

A Classic Defender with chassis rot, bulkhead corrosion and structural issues is not worth an engine replacement. The engine cost is only one part of the equation — if the chassis needs £3,000–£5,000 of welding, the total bill exceeds 60% of the vehicle's value on most examples. Our scrap value guide explains how to calculate the honest figure.

🚫

Any Defender Below £6,000 Trade Value

Below this line, almost no engine fault clears the 60% threshold. Selling for parts or to a specialist breaker is usually the financially honest outcome — Most Expensive Land Rover Repairs shows where this threshold gets tested hardest.

Our Verdict: Is It Worth Replacing a Defender Engine?

Based on the full 2025 dataset — 9,000+ real enquiries, 24,650 quote requests, and hundreds of specialist interviews — here is our definitive answer.

The short verdict:

Yes, if you own a Classic Defender (300Tdi, Td5, V8) in good structural condition. Values are strong enough to absorb the replacement cost, and these engines are well-understood and rebuildable.

Yes, if you own an L663 Defender. Values are so high that the replacement cost is a fraction of the vehicle's worth.

Maybe, if you own an L316 Defender (2.4/2.2 TDCi). The answer depends on the exact value and condition of your vehicle — use the Repair vs Replace Calculator with your own valuation.

No, if your Defender has extensive corrosion. Chassis rot and bulkhead failure are often the real reason to scrap — not the engine. Always assess the whole vehicle.

🏆

Best-Case Defenders to Own

  • 1. Classic 300Tdi (rot-free) — robust, rebuildable, strong values
  • 2. Classic Td5 (rot-free) — well-understood, good parts availability
  • 3. L663 D200/D240 — very high values, replacement cost is a fraction of vehicle value
  • 4. Classic Rover V8 — simple, parts cheap, strong values
🚫

Worst-Case Defenders to Own

  • 1. L316 2.2 TDCi (corroded chassis) — chassis rot often writes the car off
  • 2. Classic Td5 (corroded bulkhead) — bulkhead replacement is expensive and often not worth it
  • 3. L316 2.4 TDCi (high-mileage, corroded) — values low enough that major repairs exceed 60%
  • 4. Classic 300Tdi (corroded chassis) — same as above; condition is the real factor

The honest conclusion: the most financially sensible Defender to own is a rot-free Classic 300Tdi — it is robust, rebuildable, and retains strong values. The least financially sensible is an L316 Defender with a corroded chassis, where the cost of chassis repair plus engine replacement often exceeds the vehicle's value.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it worth replacing a Defender engine?

It depends on your generation and vehicle condition. Classic Defenders (300Tdi, Td5, V8) in good structural condition are almost always worth repairing. L663 Defenders are almost always worth repairing. L316 Defenders are marginal — the answer depends on the exact value and condition of your vehicle.

How much does it cost to replace a Defender engine?

Supply-only costs range from £2,000–£3,500 for a Duratorq 2.2 TDCi to £6,500–£9,500 for a 306DT D300/D350. Fitted costs add 8–20 hours labour at £65–£75/hr. Full data is in our Replacement Cost by Engine Code table.

Which Defender engine is most reliable?

The 300Tdi is legendary for reliability — it's a simple, mechanically injected diesel with no electronics. The Td5 is also robust but has a known injector loom vulnerability. The 204DTD Ingenium diesel in the L663 is the most powerful and refined but has the same timing chain caveats as other Ingenium units. Read our 300Tdi Knowledge Centre and Td5 Knowledge Centre for full detail.

Should I replace a Td5 engine or buy a new Defender?

If your Td5 Classic is in good structural condition, replace the engine — a £4,000–£6,000 repair on a £12,000–£18,000 vehicle is 30–50% (safe). A new L663 Defender starts at £50,000+. The economics are completely different. But if your Td5 has extensive chassis rot, the honest answer is to walk away.

What is the 60% rule for Defender engine replacement?

The 60% rule is our repair-to-value threshold: if the fitted replacement cost is more than 60% of your Defender's current market value, the honest verdict is usually to scrap or sell for parts. Full explanation is on our Repair, Replace or Scrap Framework page.

Is a Defender L316 2.2 TDCi worth repairing?

If the vehicle has no significant corrosion and is worth £10,000+, yes. If the chassis is corroded or the vehicle is worth less than £8,000, the answer is often no. Our scrap value guide explains how to calculate the honest figure.
