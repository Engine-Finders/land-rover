368DT TDV8 Turbo & Injector Failure — Symptoms, Cost & Repair Verdict

The 3.6-litre TDV8 – known as the 368DT – is a powerful engine, but its twin-turbo setup and piezo injectors are prone to wear, carbon buildup, and electrical failure. When they go wrong, you lose power, economy, and potentially the whole engine. This guide covers the symptoms, causes, diagnostic steps, and the honest decision framework for repair or replacement.

The Short Answer

Turbocharger and injector failure on the 368DT TDV8 is a common cause of power loss, excessive smoke, and poor fuel economy. The variable-geometry turbo actuators seize or the piezo injectors become clogged or electrically faulty. Repair costs vary – a turbo rebuild or replacement can be £1,000–£2,500, while a full set of injectors is £1,200–£2,000 plus labour. Catching it early prevents secondary damage to the DPF and catalytic converter.

Symptom Progression — What Owners Notice, In Order

Turbo and injector issues rarely appear overnight. Here's what you'll typically experience, from the first hint to a full breakdown.

  1. Stage 1 – Reduced power and sluggish acceleration. The engine feels flat, especially when overtaking or climbing hills. The turbo doesn't spool as it should.
  2. Stage 2 – Excessive black smoke. A puff of black smoke on hard acceleration, or a constant haze under load, indicates over‑fuelling – often from a sticking injector or boost leak.
  3. Stage 3 – Harsh idle or misfire. The engine may run unevenly at idle, with a noticeable vibration or periodic stumble, particularly when cold.
  4. Stage 4 – Check Engine Light. The engine management light illuminates, often with codes like P0234 (turbo overboost), P0299 (underboost), or P0263/P0272 (injector balance faults).
  5. Stage 5 – DPF blockage and limp mode. Unburnt fuel from faulty injectors clogs the DPF, causing frequent regenerations and eventually a restricted performance warning. The car may enter limp mode.
  6. Stage 6 – Turbo failure. If the actuator seizes or the turbine shaft snaps, you'll hear a grinding noise, lose all boost, and may see metallic particles in the oil.

If you notice any of these, don't ignore them. A failing injector can wash the cylinder bores and dilute the oil, leading to catastrophic engine failure. For more on turbo issues, see our turbocharger failure guide.

Root Cause — Why Turbos and Injectors Fail on the 368DT

The 368DT is a 3.6‑litre V8 twin‑turbo diesel. While robust, its common‑rail injection and variable‑geometry turbo are sensitive to oil quality, fuel contamination, and carbon buildup. The primary failure modes are:

  • Turbo actuator seizure. The variable vanes stick due to soot and carbon, causing overboost or underboost. This stresses the turbo and can damage the turbine shaft.
  • Injector clogging and electrical failure. The piezo injectors have tiny nozzles that can block with fuel contaminants, or the internal electronics can fail, leading to poor spray pattern and over‑fuelling.
  • Oil contamination. Used oil with high soot levels can gum up the turbo bearings and actuator. Infrequent oil changes accelerate this.
  • Fuel quality issues. UK diesel often has high bio‑content, which can leave deposits on injectors and cause corrosion over time.
  • DPF regeneration stress. Frequent forced regenerations raise exhaust temperatures, which can damage the turbo and injector seals over time.

The most common culprit is the turbo actuator – a replacement often cures boost issues. Injector problems usually require coding after replacement. For a detailed breakdown of the 368DT, see our 368DT Knowledge Centre.

What Makes Turbo and Injector Failure More or Less Likely

Oil Change History

Regular oil changes (every 6,000–8,000 miles) with the correct spec (5W‑30 or 0W‑30) keep turbo bearings and actuators clean. Extended intervals allow sludge to form.

Using premium diesel with higher cetane and detergent additives reduces injector deposits. Avoid supermarket fuel if possible.

Short trips prevent the engine from reaching operating temperature, leading to carbon buildup and DPF sooting. Regular motorway runs help burn off deposits.

How a Specialist Confirms Turbo and Injector Faults

Diagnosing these issues requires a combination of scanning, live data, and physical inspection. A good specialist will follow this sequence.

1

Diagnostic Scan

Using a Land Rover‑compatible tool, the specialist reads fault codes. Common ones include P0234 (turbo overboost), P0299 (underboost), P0263–P0272 (injector cylinder balance), and P0401 (EGR, often linked).

2

Live Data – Boost & Fuel Trim

With the engine running, the technician monitors boost pressure (actual vs. requested), injector correction values, and fuel rail pressure. Deviations point to the faulty component.

3

Turbo Actuator Test

The actuator can be commanded to move via the diagnostic tool. If it fails to respond or sticks, the turbo needs attention.

4

Injector Test – Leak‑off & Return Flow

A leak‑off test measures the amount of fuel returning from each injector. Excessive return indicates a worn injector. This is a simple and accurate test.

5

Oil and Fuel Filter Inspection

Cutting open the oil filter can reveal metal particles from turbo bearing wear. The fuel filter may show signs of contamination or water.

Prevention & Early Detection — What You Can Do

Prevention is always cheaper than a turbo rebuild. Here's how to protect your 368DT.

  • Change oil and filter every 6,000–8,000 miles. Use a high‑quality 5W‑30 or 0W‑30 that meets Ford or JLR spec. Soot and sludge are the turbo's worst enemies.
  • Use premium diesel regularly. Shell V‑Power or BP Ultimate contain detergents that clean injectors.
  • Drive the car hard occasionally. A good motorway run (30+ minutes) at 70mph allows the DPF to regenerate and clears carbon from the turbo vanes.
  • Check for boost leaks. Listen for hissing noises and look for split hoses – a boost leak makes the turbo work harder and can cause over‑speed.
  • Listen for unusual noises. A whine or grinding from the turbo is a sign of bearing wear – have it inspected immediately.

Prevention is much cheaper than repair. For model‑specific service schedules, see our Range Rover service schedule.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of turbo failure on a 368DT?

A loss of power, a whining or grinding noise, and black smoke under acceleration are the early warnings. A check engine light with P0299 (underboost) or P0234 (overboost) is also common.

Can I drive with a faulty injector or turbo?

It is strongly advised against. A faulty injector can wash the cylinder bore, dilute the oil, and cause catastrophic engine failure. A failing turbo can disintegrate and send debris through the engine. Have it towed to a specialist.

How much does a 368DT turbo replacement cost in the UK in 2025?

At an independent specialist, expect £1,200–£2,200 [EM-QUOTE] for a reconditioned turbo (supply only) plus 6–10 hours labour. Main dealer prices are significantly higher.

Is it worth replacing the injectors on a high‑mileage 368DT?

Yes – a fresh set of injectors restores fuel economy, power, and reduces DPF regens. If the rest of the vehicle is in good condition, it's a worthwhile investment.

What causes the turbo actuator to stick?

Soot and carbon buildup from the EGR and DPF systems accumulate on the variable‑vane mechanism. Regular motorway driving and using premium fuel can help prevent this.

Are there any recalls for 368DT turbo or injector issues?

Land Rover has issued some TSBs for the 368DT, but no major recalls. Check with your dealer using your VIN to see if any apply.
