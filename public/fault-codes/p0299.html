P0299 Land Rover — Turbocharger Underboost

The ECU has detected that boost pressure is lower than commanded – a classic symptom of a turbocharger fault, a boost leak, or a stuck wastegate. We break down the causes from cheapest to most expensive, so you know where to start and when it's safe to drive.

P0299 means the turbocharger isn't producing enough boost pressure. On Land Rover diesels – especially 204DTD Ingenium and 306DT V6 – this is often a simple boost leak or a stuck wastegate, but can also point to a failing turbo actuator or a blocked DPF.

  • Most common and cheapest fix: a split boost hose or loose intercooler pipe – typically £100–£250 fitted [PATTERN DATA]
  • Worst case: turbocharger replacement, costing £800–£2,000+ [PATTERN DATA]
  • Often accompanied by limp mode, engine management light, and turbo whistle
  • Frequently appears with P0234 (overboost) if the wastegate is sticking intermittently

Do not replace the turbo before ruling out boost leaks and vacuum issues. Follow the cheapest-first diagnostic order below.

What P0299 Actually Means

The turbocharger uses exhaust gases to spin a turbine, which drives a compressor that forces extra air into the engine. The ECU monitors boost pressure via a sensor (MAP or boost pressure sensor) and compares it to a target value based on engine speed, load, and driver demand. P0299 is set when actual boost pressure falls significantly below the target for a set period. This underboost can be caused by a physical leak in the intake or charge air system (e.g., a split hose, loose clamp, or cracked intercooler), a faulty wastegate or actuator that is stuck open, a blocked exhaust (DPF or cat), or a failing turbocharger that can no longer produce the required pressure. On Land Rover engines, boost leaks are particularly common on the 204DTD Ingenium and 306DT V6 due to the complex plumbing of the charge air cooler and the use of plastic pipes that can crack with age.

Symptoms You'll Notice Alongside It

  • Engine management light illuminated – usually amber
  • Limp mode or noticeably reduced power – the engine feels flat, especially when accelerating
  • Whistling or hissing noise from the engine bay, especially under load – often a boost leak
  • Poor acceleration and sluggish response – the car struggles to climb hills or overtake
  • Increased fuel consumption – the ECU compensates with more fuel to maintain power
  • In severe cases, black smoke from the exhaust – over‑fuelling due to lack of air

Diagnostic Order — Cheapest First

1

Visual inspection of charge air system

Check all boost hoses, clamps, and the intercooler for cracks, splits, or loose connections. Often the problem is obvious.

2

Perform a smoke test or pressure test

A smoke machine can find leaks that aren't visible. A pressure test with a regulated air supply is also effective.

3

Check wastegate actuator operation

Apply vacuum or use live data to see if the actuator moves freely. A stuck actuator is a common cause.

4

Exhaust backpressure check

Measure pressure before and after the DPF – if it's excessively high, the DPF is blocked.

5

Only then consider the turbocharger

If everything else is clear, the turbo itself may be worn – but this is the least common outcome in our data.

Can You Still Drive It?

Yes, but with reduced performance and increased risk. P0299 itself isn't an immediate engine‑killer, but driving with a boost leak can cause the ECU to over‑fuel, which may damage the DPF and catalytic converter over time. If the underboost is caused by a blocked DPF, continued driving can lead to a complete DPF failure – a much larger bill. If you notice black smoke, a strong smell of diesel, or the engine is in limp mode, get it checked promptly. It's safe to drive gently to a specialist, but avoid heavy acceleration and long motorway journeys until the cause is identified.

Related Fault Codes

P0299 is often accompanied by other turbo or intake codes. The combination can pinpoint the exact problem.

P0234 – Turbocharger Overboost

If both P0299 and P0234 appear intermittently, suspect a sticky wastegate actuator or a faulty boost control solenoid.

P0045 – Turbocharger Boost Control Solenoid Circuit

Points to an electrical fault in the boost control circuit – often a wiring issue or failed solenoid.

P2463 – DPF Soot Accumulation

If present, the DPF is likely blocked, causing exhaust restriction and underboost – see DPF warning light.

P0087 – Fuel Rail Pressure Too Low

May co‑occur if the engine is in limp mode and fuel pressure is affected – see turbocharger failure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drive with P0299 on a Land Rover?

Yes, but only gently and for short distances. If the car is in limp mode or you see black smoke, avoid heavy acceleration. A boost leak can cause over‑fuelling, damaging the DPF, so get it looked at as soon as possible.

Does P0299 always mean the turbo has failed?

No – in most cases it's a boost leak, a stuck actuator, or a blocked DPF. Turbo failure is the least common cause. Follow the diagnostic order above to avoid an expensive misdiagnosis.

Will clearing the code fix it?

Only temporarily. The code will return as soon as the ECU detects underboost again. Clearing it also wipes freeze‑frame data that would help a specialist diagnose the root cause.

How much should P0299 diagnosis cost?

Expect 1–2 hours of diagnostic time at an independent JLR specialist – around £100–£200, often offset against the repair if you proceed with them.

What's the most common fix for P0299 on a Discovery Sport or Evoque?

For the 204DTD Ingenium, a split boost hose or a loose intercooler pipe is the most common and cheapest fix – it resolves the code in over 40% of cases [PATTERN DATA]. Always inspect the charge air system first.
