P132B Land Rover — Turbocharger Boost Control Position Sensor

A common diagnostic trouble code indicating that the turbocharger boost control position sensor is reporting an incorrect or out-of-range value. Often a sensor or wiring issue — but if ignored, it can lead to overboost, underboost, or even turbocharger failure.

P132B means the ECU has detected that the turbocharger boost control position sensor signal is out of range — either the sensor is reporting a position that doesn't match the commanded position, or the sensor itself is faulty. On Land Rover diesels (especially the 204DTD Ingenium and 306DT V6), this is usually a sensor or wiring issue, not a failed turbocharger. Diagnose cheapest-first to avoid unnecessary replacement.

  • Most common real cause: a faulty boost control position sensor — typically £150–£350 fitted [PATTERN DATA]
  • Worst case: turbocharger replacement due to seized vanes or actuator, £1,200–£2,000+ [PATTERN DATA]
  • Commonly seen alongside engine management light and limp mode
  • Frequently appears with P0299 or P0234 — the combination helps narrow the cause

Do not let anyone sell you a new turbocharger before the sensor, wiring, and actuator have been ruled out. Full diagnostic order below.

What P132B Actually Means

The turbocharger boost control system uses a position sensor to monitor the actual position of the turbocharger's actuator — which controls the variable geometry vanes (on VGT turbos) or the wastegate (on fixed-geometry turbos). The ECU commands a specific actuator position based on engine load, RPM, and boost demand, and then checks that the sensor's reported position matches the command. P132B is logged when the sensor signal is implausible — either the voltage is out of the expected range (too high or too low), the reported position doesn't change when commanded, or the reported position doesn't match the commanded position within a tolerance. Crucially, the code describes a signal or position mismatch, not a component failure. The sensor itself could be faulty, the wiring could be damaged, the actuator could be stuck (due to carbon buildup on a VGT turbo), or the turbocharger vanes could be seized. On 204DTD Ingenium and 306DT V6 engines, carbon buildup in the variable geometry mechanism is a well-documented issue that can cause the actuator to stick, triggering this code. The distinction between a £150 sensor and a £2,000 turbocharger is why the diagnostic order below is critical.

Symptoms You'll Notice Alongside It

  • Engine management light illuminated, usually amber
  • Limp mode or reduced engine power — the ECU may limit boost to protect the engine
  • Noticeable lack of power, especially under acceleration or when towing
  • Black smoke from the exhaust (over-fuelling due to low boost)
  • Turbo whistle or unusual noises from the turbocharger area
  • Poor fuel economy — the engine runs richer to compensate for reduced boost
  • Restricted performance message on the dash

Diagnostic Order — Cheapest First

1

Check sensor connector and wiring

Inspect the boost control position sensor connector for corrosion, bent pins, or damaged wires. Check for correct 5V reference, ground, and signal continuity.

2

Live-data the sensor output

A specialist can read the sensor voltage and commanded position via live data. With the ignition on, engine off, the sensor should read a stable value corresponding to the rest position. If the value is erratic or out of range, the sensor is faulty.

3

Command the actuator with a diagnostic tool

Using a bi-directional scan tool, command the actuator through its full range of motion. Watch the sensor feedback. If the position doesn't change, the actuator or wiring is faulty. If it moves but the sensor reading is inconsistent, the sensor is faulty.

4

Inspect the actuator and turbo vanes

If the sensor and wiring are confirmed good, remove the actuator and manually check the vane movement on the turbo. If the vanes are stiff or stuck, the turbo may need cleaning or replacement.

5

Only then consider turbocharger replacement

If the sensor, wiring, actuator, and vanes all check out and the code persists, the turbocharger itself may be mechanically worn.

Can You Still Drive It?

Conditionally, yes — but get it checked soon. P132B on its own is not an immediate engine-destroying fault. However, driving with a faulty boost control system can lead to overboost (which can damage the engine or turbo) or underboost (which causes poor performance and increased fuel consumption). If the car has entered limp mode, you can drive to a garage but avoid heavy acceleration, towing, or motorway driving. If you notice black smoke or the turbo making unusual noises, get it recovered. In most cases, if the engine management light is steady (not flashing) and the car is driving reasonably, you can drive a short distance to a garage within a few days.

Related Fault Codes

P132B rarely appears alone. Which codes accompany it materially changes the diagnosis.

P0299 — Turbocharger Underboost

If both P132B and P0299 appear, the turbo is likely not producing enough boost — often due to stuck vanes or a failed actuator.

P0234 — Turbocharger Overboost

If both P132B and P0234 appear, the boost control system may be stuck in a high-boost position — dangerous for the engine.

P0243 — Turbocharger Wastegate Solenoid A

Indicates a solenoid circuit fault — often a wiring issue that may also affect the position sensor.

P0045 — Turbocharger Boost Control Solenoid Circuit

Another circuit-related code that often appears alongside P132B.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I drive with P132B on a Land Rover?

Yes, for short journeys to get it diagnosed — but avoid heavy acceleration, towing, or motorway driving. If the car goes into limp mode or you hear unusual turbo noises, get it recovered. Prolonged driving can damage the turbo or the engine.

Does P132B always mean the turbocharger has failed?

No. In most cases, the issue is a faulty sensor, damaged wiring, or carbon buildup on the VGT vanes. The turbocharger itself is often still in good condition. Don't let anyone sell you a new turbo before the basics have been checked.

Can carbon buildup cause P132B?

Yes, especially on variable geometry turbochargers (VGT). Carbon deposits can cause the vanes to stick, preventing the actuator from moving freely. This is common on higher-mileage 306DT and 204DTD engines.

How much should diagnosis cost?

Expect one to two hours of diagnostic time at an independent JLR specialist — around £80–£160. Many will offset this against the repair if you proceed.

What is the difference between P132B and P0299?

P0299 is an underboost code — the ECU detects that boost pressure is lower than expected. P132B is a position sensor code — the sensor reading is out of range or doesn't match the commanded position. P132B is often the root cause that leads to underboost.
