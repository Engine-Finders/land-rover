4.4 TT V8 Timing Chain or Belt? Service Life & Interval

The 4.4‑litre twin‑turbo V8 (used across L405, L494 and select L322 models) is chain‑driven. This page gives the service interval, replacement cost bands, and the real‑world caveats that change the answer.

The 4.4‑litre twin‑turbo V8 (also known as the 4.4 SDV8 / 508PS) uses a timing chain – not a belt. The manufacturer does not specify a fixed replacement interval, but real‑world specialist guidance points to inspection at 100,000–120,000 miles and proactive replacement if the tensioner shows signs of wear or the engine exhibits a cold‑start rattle.

  • Chain replacement supply cost (parts only): £600–£1,200 [EM‑QUOTE]
  • Labour hours: 12–16 (indie) / 18–22 (main dealer) [EM‑INTERVIEW]
  • Common misconception: this is not a belt – using belt‑service intervals on a chain engine is a common mistake.
  • Last verified: February 2026

Notes & Caveats

The 4.4 TT V8 chain system is robust, but two factors dramatically affect its lifespan:

  • Oil quality and change intervals – using the correct grade (0W‑30 or 5W‑30 low‑SAPS) and changing it every 12 months or 10,000 miles (whichever comes first) is critical. Extended intervals (e.g., 16,000 miles) allow chain tensioner wear to accelerate.
  • Short‑journey driving – frequent cold starts and short trips prevent the engine from reaching full operating temperature, which increases chain and tensioner wear. This is the same pattern seen on the 204DTD Ingenium diesel (see oil dilution page), although the mechanism is different – here it’s about oil viscosity and tensioner pressure.
  • Specialists we surveyed [EM‑INTERVIEW] recommend inspecting the chain at 100,000 miles and replacing the tensioner as a precaution if any rattle is heard on cold start. The cost of a new tensioner is significantly less than a full chain replacement if it fails and causes valve timing issues.

Common Mistakes

  • Assuming it’s a belt. The 4.4 TT V8 is chain‑driven – using a belt service interval (e.g., 60k miles) is irrelevant. The chain itself does not have a specified replacement interval, but tensioners do wear.
  • Ignoring cold‑start rattle. A brief rattle on startup is often the tensioner not holding oil pressure. Ignoring it leads to stretched chain and eventual timing drift, which can cause valve‑to‑piston contact. This is covered in depth on our Timing Chain Diagnosis Guide.
  • Using the wrong oil viscosity. Thicker oil (e.g., 10W‑40) can slow tensioner actuation, increasing wear. Always use 0W‑30 or 5W‑30 meeting JLR’s low‑SAPS specification.
  • Confusing with the 3.6 TDV8 (368DT). The 3.6 TDV8 also uses a chain, but its tensioner design is different and more prone to failure. See our 368DT engine page for the distinction.
  • Replacing only the chain without the tensioner. Many specialists advise replacing both as a set – the tensioner is the usual wear item, and a new chain on an old tensioner can fail prematurely.

How to Verify on the Actual Vehicle

The 4.4 TT V8 has a clearly marked timing chain cover with the engine code and build date stamped on the cylinder block. Use our Engine Number Location guide to confirm your exact engine code (e.g., 508PS vs 448DT). A visual inspection of the chain tensioner requires removing the front cover – a job best left to a specialist. However, you can listen for a cold‑start rattle: if it lasts more than 1–2 seconds, the tensioner is likely losing oil pressure and needs attention. For a definitive diagnosis, a mechanic can use a stethoscope or remove the camshaft position sensor to inspect chain slack.
